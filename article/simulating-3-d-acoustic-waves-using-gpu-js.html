Simulating 3D Acoustic Waves Using gpu.js

Simulating acoustic sound wave dispersion across complex 3D rooms using gpu.js is technically feasible, but it is bound by significant performance and architectural constraints. By leveraging WebGL through gpu.js, developers can parallelize the mathematical updates required for wave propagation models like the Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD) method directly in JavaScript. However, while simple or low-resolution spaces can be rendered effectively, high-frequency dispersion and intricate 3D architectural boundaries present steep memory and computational challenges in a browser-based environment.

The Mechanism: Implementing FDTD with gpu.js

To simulate true acoustic wave dispersion—including phenomena like diffraction, interference, and reflection—the standard approach is the 3D Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD) method. FDTD discretizes both space and time, solving the acoustic wave equation across a 3D grid of nodes.

Because gpu.js transpiles a subset of JavaScript into GLSL shaders, it can execute grid-wide matrix calculations in parallel. In a 3D FDTD model:

  • The room volume is broken down into a 3D voxel grid.
  • Each node stores acoustic pressure and particle velocity.
  • gpu.js kernels update each cell’s state at time step \(t+1\) based on its neighboring values at time step \(t\).

By flattening 3D spatial coordinates into 2D textures or utilizing supported 3D array inputs, gpu.js handles the matrix math significantly faster than standard JavaScript loops.

Challenges with Complex 3D Geometries

While empty rectangular rooms are straightforward to compute, real-world rooms introduce complexities that push gpu.js to its limits:

  1. Boundary Conditions and Voxelization: Realistic simulation requires defining absorbent materials, non-parallel walls, furniture, and apertures. Non-planar surfaces must be voxelized into stair-stepped cells, which can cause artificial scattering unless smoothed with complex impedance boundary conditions. Encoding these conditional rules within gpu.js kernels increases shader branching, which degrades GPU parallel processing performance.

  2. Memory Scaling (\(O(N^3)\)): Acoustic accuracy requires at least 6 to 10 spatial nodes per wavelength to prevent numerical dispersion. High-frequency sounds have short wavelengths (e.g., 1 kHz corresponds to ~34 cm, while 10 kHz is ~3.4 cm). Simulating a medium-sized room (e.g., \(10\text{m} \times 10\text{m} \times 3\text{m}\)) at higher frequencies requires tens to hundreds of millions of cells. Storing multiple floating-point state buffers for this volume easily exceeds WebGL texture dimensions and browser memory limits.

  3. Compute Constraints vs. Modern WebGPU: gpu.js relies on WebGL fragment shaders, which lack compute shader features such as shared workgroup memory and atomic operations. Calculating 3D neighborhoods (stencil operations) requires redundant memory lookups from global texture memory, reducing efficiency compared to modern compute APIs.

Alternative Approaches: Geometric Acoustics

If the goal is to evaluate room impulse responses (RIR) or sound localization rather than wave-level physical phenomena like phase cancellation and diffraction, geometric acoustics offers a more viable alternative within gpu.js.

Techniques like ray tracing, beam tracing, or acoustic radiosity approximate sound as particles or rays. gpu.js can trace thousands of sound rays in parallel, calculating wall intersections, absorption coefficients, and arrival times at a listener's position. This approach handles complex 3D meshes much better than volumetric grids and requires significantly less memory.

Practical Verdict

gpu.js is capable of simulating 3D acoustic wave dispersion for:

  • Low-frequency waves (infrasound to roughly 500 Hz).
  • Small, simplified spatial enclosures.
  • Educational demonstrations and proof-of-concept visualizers.

For full-spectrum, production-grade acoustic analysis of complex architectural designs, gpu.js is generally outmatched by native CUDA/C++ engines or newer web standards like WebGPU, which offer the compute shaders and memory bandwidth required for high-resolution volumetric acoustics.
