Audio Impulse Response Convolution in gpu.js

Executing room acoustic impulse response (RIR) convolution with tens of thousands of samples is technically possible in gpu.js, but it comes with severe practical limitations depending on whether the application requires real-time or offline processing. While gpu.js leverages the GPU via WebGL to perform parallel mathematical operations, time-domain convolution at this scale creates massive computational loads, and frequency-domain alternatives face structural constraints within the library. This article analyzes the feasibility, performance bottlenecks, and architectural constraints of running large-scale audio convolution in gpu.js.

The Computational Scale of Impulse Responses

A typical room impulse response sampled at 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz spanning just 1 to 2 seconds contains between 44,100 and 96,000 samples. Direct time-domain convolution requires multiplying and accumulating every sample of the input audio with every sample of the impulse response:

\[\text{Operations} = N \times M\]

For an audio block of 44,100 samples and an impulse response of 44,100 samples, direct convolution requires approximately 1.94 billion floating-point operations. While the massively parallel architecture of a GPU can compute these output samples simultaneously, executing billions of operations per block strains standard WebGL shader passes and easily triggers WebGL context loss or driver timeout detection (TDR) in browsers.

Time-Domain vs. Frequency-Domain in gpu.js

Standard audio engines bypass direct convolution by using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithms to perform convolution in the frequency domain (via Overlap-Add or Overlap-Save methods), reducing the computational complexity from \(O(N \times M)\) to \(O(N \log N)\).

Implementing partitioned FFT-based convolution entirely inside gpu.js is exceedingly complex. Gpu.js compiles a subset of JavaScript into GLSL shaders, primarily designed for simple map-reduce and matrix operations. It lacks native support for complex numbers, bit-reversal sorting, and multi-pass butterfly networks required for high-performance GPU-based FFTs. Consequently, developers in gpu.js are often forced into brute-force, time-domain convolution, which scales poorly as the impulse response size grows past a few thousand samples.

The Real-Time Bottleneck: WebGL Readback Latency

Even if a modern GPU can compute the time-domain convolution quickly enough, audio processing generally operates under strict real-time deadlines (buffer sizes of 128 to 512 samples, or 2.6 to 11.6 milliseconds of latency).

Gpu.js operates on top of WebGL, which introduces two major transfer bottlenecks:

  1. Host-to-Device Transfer: Converting raw Float32 audio buffers into WebGL textures.
  2. Device-to-Host Readback: Calling gl.readPixels() to extract the processed audio data back to the CPU.

WebGL readbacks are inherently synchronous and block the main JavaScript thread, introducing latency spikes of 10 to 30 milliseconds or more. This transfer latency exceeds standard real-time audio tolerances, resulting in severe buffer under-runs and audible glitches.

Offline and Batch Processing Feasibility

For non-real-time audio processing—such as offline reverb rendering, scientific simulation, or spatial acoustics preprocessing—gpu.js can execute large impulse response convolutions successfully. By dividing the audio signal into smaller chunks or computing samples across a 2D texture grid, gpu.js avoids browser watchdog timeouts and computes large-scale direct convolution significantly faster than single-threaded CPU JavaScript.

Alternatives for Production Audio

For applications requiring convolution with tens of thousands of samples, alternate web standards offer superior performance:

  • Web Audio API (ConvolverNode): Highly optimized, written in C++, uses SIMD and partitioned FFT algorithms natively with zero WebGL overhead.
  • WebAssembly (Wasm) + SIMD: Enables custom partitioned convolution algorithms directly on the audio worklet thread without GPU memory transfer penalties.
  • WebGPU: The successor to WebGL provides compute shaders and shared memory, allowing true fast Fourier transform pipelines and asynchronous buffer mapping without the limitations of gpu.js.
