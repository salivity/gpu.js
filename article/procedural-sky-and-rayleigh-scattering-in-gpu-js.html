Procedural Sky and Rayleigh Scattering in GPU.js

Procedural sky rendering based on atmospheric Rayleigh scattering models can be successfully implemented using gpu.js. By compiling JavaScript functions into WebGL fragment shaders, gpu.js allows developers to execute the heavy raymarching and numerical integration required for atmospheric simulation directly on the graphics card. While dedicated graphics frameworks like Three.js or raw GLSL are traditionally used for these tasks, gpu.js provides a viable, pure-JavaScript approach to compute light scattering values per pixel and render dynamic, physically inspired skies directly to an HTML5 canvas.

The Physics of Rayleigh Scattering

Rayleigh scattering describes the elastic scattering of light by particles much smaller than the wavelength of the light, such as molecules in Earth's atmosphere. This phenomenon accounts for the blue color of the daytime sky and the red, orange hues seen at sunrise and sunset.

To render a procedural sky, the engine must simulate two primary components:

  1. Out-scattering and Extinction: As sunlight traverses the atmosphere toward the camera, portions of the light are scattered away. Shorter wavelengths (blue light) scatter significantly more than longer wavelengths (red light), adhering to the relationship \(I \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^4}\).
  2. In-scattering: Light traveling from the sun enters the view ray toward the camera after scattering off air molecules. Computing this requires evaluating a phase function, typically defined for Rayleigh scattering as: \[P(\theta) = \frac{3}{16\pi}(1 + \cos^2\theta)\] where \(\theta\) is the angle between the sun's rays and the view direction.

Implementing Scattering in GPU.js

To render this model in gpu.js, you utilize a graphical kernel. When initialized with { graphical: true }, gpu.js binds the execution output to an HTML canvas, treating each kernel thread as a pixel renderer analogous to a fragment shader.

The procedural pipeline operates as follows:

  • Ray Generation: For every pixel on the canvas, compute a normalized view ray vector pointing outward from the camera's position into the hemisphere.
  • Atmosphere Intersection: Calculate the intersection distances between the view ray and the spherical shells representing the planet's surface and the top of the atmosphere.
  • Raymarching: Sample multiple points along the primary view ray between the viewer and the atmosphere boundary.
  • Optical Depth Calculation: At each primary sample point, cast a secondary ray toward the sun to compute the optical density and determine how much sunlight reaches that point without being scattered away.
  • Integration and Color Output: Accumulate the in-scattered light scaled by the Rayleigh phase function and atmospheric density. Finally, assign the result using this.color(r, g, b, 1) to render the pixel.

Technical Limitations and Workarounds

While gpu.js can handle the mathematical calculations, certain architectural constraints must be addressed:

  • Fixed Loop Boundaries: gpu.js requires loops to have static, compile-time bounds. Dynamic step counts for raymarching are not supported, meaning the number of integration steps along the view ray and sun ray must remain constant.
  • Limited Data Types: gpu.js translates JavaScript primitives to GLSL. Because custom structs and complex object orientations are unsupported inside the kernel, mathematical vectors (such as 3D ray directions) must be flattened into separate numerical variables or 1D arrays.
  • Scene Integration: gpu.js generates output directly to its own canvas context. Integrating an atmospheric model generated via gpu.js into an existing 3D scene (such as one with terrain and geometry) requires either drawing the sky as a pre-computed background texture or generating dynamic cubemaps/lookup tables (LUTs) that another WebGL context can consume.

Conclusion

gpu.js is fully capable of rendering procedural skies and calculating Rayleigh scattering models. It offers an accessible bridge for developers who want to harness GPU-accelerated raymarching without writing raw WebGL boilerplate. For standalone sky domes, atmospheric lookup table generation, or real-time 2D horizon simulations, gpu.js provides sufficient performance and mathematical precision.
