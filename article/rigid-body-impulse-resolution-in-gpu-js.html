Rigid Body Impulse Resolution in gpu.js

Resolving contact impulses across multiple colliding rigid bodies in gpu.js is technically feasible but severely constrained by the architecture of WebGL fragment shaders. This article examines the mechanics of parallel impulse resolution, the execution model of gpu.js, the algorithmic trade-offs required to resolve simultaneous contacts, and the practical viability of using gpu.js for multi-body physics engines.

The Challenge of Multi-Body Impulse Resolution

Standard rigid body physics engines typically resolve velocity constraints at contact points using an iterative solver, such as Projected Gauss-Seidel (PGS) or Sequential Impulses (SI). In Sequential Impulses, contacts are processed one by one. When an impulse is applied to resolve penetration or relative velocity between two colliding bodies, the linear and angular velocities of both bodies are updated immediately. Subsequent contact calculations involving either of those bodies rely on those updated velocities.

This dependency chain creates an inherently sequential process. When two objects collide with each other while simultaneously colliding with other bodies or the ground, resolving these contacts concurrently without synchronization leads to data races, energy creation, or instability.

gpu.js Execution Model and Limitations

gpu.js compiles a subset of JavaScript into WebGL shaders, running parallel operations primarily through fragment shaders rendered to textures. This design introduces specific constraints when applied to physics solvers:

  1. No Native Atomic Operations: Standard WebGL 1.0 and 2.0 fragment shaders cannot perform atomic read-modify-write operations across shared memory. A fragment can only write to its own designated output pixel.
  2. Scatter vs. Gather: Shaders in gpu.js excel at "gather" operations (reading multiple inputs to determine one output) rather than "scatter" operations (writing results from one contact calculation to modify two arbitrary bodies).
  3. Pipelining Overhead: Iterative algorithms require ping-ponging framebuffers. Each iteration of an impulse solver must run as a separate kernel pass, reading from the previous state texture and writing to a new one. This introduces substantial GPU-to-GPU dispatch overhead.

Algorithmic Approaches in gpu.js

To resolve multi-body contacts in gpu.js, the algorithm must avoid sequential write dependencies. Two main approaches can be implemented:

1. Jacobi-Style Solvers

Instead of Sequential Impulses (Gauss-Seidel), a Jacobi iteration can be used:

  • Each contact evaluates its required impulse based purely on the velocities from the start of the current iteration.
  • A secondary kernel gathers all accumulated impulses affecting each rigid body and updates the bodies simultaneously.
  • The solver alternates between these two kernels across multiple iterations.

While easily parallelized in gpu.js, Jacobi solvers suffer from poor convergence, numerical stiffness, and instability during complex multi-body resting contacts or tall stacks.

2. Contact Graph Coloring

To maintain the stability of Gauss-Seidel iterations, contacts can be partitioned into independent sets ("colors") where no two contacts in the same set share a common rigid body:

  • All contacts within a single color set are resolved in parallel via a single gpu.js kernel pass.
  • The simulation iterates through each color set sequentially.

Constructing and dynamically updating the contact graph coloring is computationally expensive. If performed on the CPU, the overhead of transferring partitioned contact batches to gpu.js often negates the performance gains of GPU execution.

Practical Assessment

While gpu.js can handle broadphase and narrowphase collision detection effectively through parallel spatial hashing, contact impulse resolution across multiple interacting objects is poorly matched to its abstraction layer.

For simple systems with sparse contacts, a Jacobi solver implemented via ping-pong render targets in gpu.js functions, but it requires high iteration counts to prevent jitter. For robust multi-body impulse resolution involving deep contact graphs, stacks, or complex constraints, direct WebGPU compute shaders with storage buffers and atomic operations are significantly better suited than the WebGL-based pipeline of gpu.js.
