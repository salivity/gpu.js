Minimum GPU.js Latency for Real-Time Audio DSP

Utilizing gpu.js for real-time live audio Digital Signal Processing (DSP) allows developers to run massive parallel operations within the browser, but it is heavily bottlenecked by WebGL architecture. In practical real-time implementations, the minimum achievable round-trip processing latency when using gpu.js sits between 20 and 50 milliseconds, with an absolute theoretical lower bound around 15 milliseconds on high-end hardware. Because standard live audio performance generally demands sub-10-millisecond latency to avoid perceptible delay, understanding the technical bottlenecks of gpu.js is critical before choosing it for audio tasks.

The Real-Time Audio Latency Budget

Standard web audio operates on an execution quantum of 128 frames. At sample rates of 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz, this window corresponds to approximately 2.67 to 2.9 milliseconds per block. Professional real-time audio systems aim for total round-trip latencies under 10 milliseconds. Within the browser, achieving this requires the dedicated, high-priority thread of the AudioWorklet API.

Why gpu.js Introduces Latency

gpu.js compiles JavaScript functions into WebGL shader code, dispatching calculations onto the GPU. While throughput for parallel mathematical tasks is exceptionally high, the round-trip execution introduces three major sources of latency:

  1. Synchronous Readback Stalls (gl.readPixels): WebGL relies on asynchronous pipelines. To return computed audio samples back to JavaScript, gpu.js must read data back from the GPU via readPixels or pixel buffer objects. This operation halts the CPU pipeline until the GPU completes all pending draw calls and transfers memory, introducing stalls that often exceed 5 to 15 milliseconds alone.
  2. Context and Thread Boundaries: Web Audio’s AudioWorkletProcessor does not natively support WebGL contexts. Audio data must be transmitted from the AudioWorklet to either the main UI thread or an offscreen web worker via postMessage, processed with gpu.js, and messaged back. This inter-thread serialization and event-loop dispatch reliably adds another 5 to 10 milliseconds of jitter and latency.
  3. Small-Batch Inefficiency: GPUs excel at millions of simultaneous floating-point calculations, not the 128 to 256 sequential samples typical of real-time audio. Sending tiny sample arrays incurs disproportionate GPU dispatch overhead.

Mitigating Latency with Buffer Trade-Offs

To prevent audio dropouts (crackling) caused by GPU pipeline stalls, developers must increase buffer sizes. Operating with buffers of 1024 or 2048 samples gives the GPU enough work to justify the transfer cost and smooths out frame-rate variations. However, a 1024-sample buffer at 48 kHz intrinsically creates 21.3 milliseconds of algorithmic latency before any data transfer or computation even begins. Combined with IPC messaging and WebGL readback overhead, total system latency routinely measures between 30 and 60 milliseconds.

Conclusion

If your application strictly requires live monitoring, microphone pitch correction, or instrument input where latency must remain imperceptible (< 10 ms), gpu.js is unsuitable due to WebGL data-transfer bottlenecks. However, for massive parallel operations—such as complex multi-band spectral analysis, high-order Ambisonics, or real-time visualization where an audio-to-visual latency of 20 to 50 milliseconds is acceptable—gpu.js provides sufficient performance when configured with buffer sizes of at least 512 to 1024 samples.
