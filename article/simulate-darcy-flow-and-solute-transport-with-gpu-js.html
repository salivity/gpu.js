Simulate Darcy Flow and Solute Transport with GPU.js

It is entirely possible to compute Darcy flow and solute transport through porous rock media using GPU.js by discretizing the governing partial differential equations onto regular grids and solving them with parallel stencil algorithms. GPU.js compiles written JavaScript functions into WebGL shader language, allowing parallel computation across millions of simulated rock pores directly on the graphics hardware. While native environments like CUDA or OpenCL are typically used for high-end geological simulations, GPU.js offers an accessible, zero-installation platform capable of executing real-time, interactive porous media simulations within modern web browsers.

Governing Equations in Porous Media

Simulating fluid dynamics and contaminant migration in geological formations requires coupling two physical processes: fluid flow and mass transport.

Darcy flow defines the velocity field of fluid moving through porous media. Under steady-state conditions in an isotropic or anisotropic medium, the pressure head distribution \(h\) is governed by the continuity equation combined with Darcy's law:

\[\nabla \cdot (K \nabla h) = 0\]

where \(K\) is the hydraulic conductivity tensor representing the rock's permeability. Once the hydraulic head field is solved, the Darcy flux vector \(\mathbf{q}\) and pore water velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) are calculated using the rock's effective porosity \(\theta\):

\[\mathbf{v} = -\frac{K}{\theta} \nabla h\]

Solute transport is governed by the Advection-Dispersion Equation (ADE), which tracks the concentration \(C\) of a chemical or contaminant over time:

\[\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{D} \nabla C) - \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{v} C)\]

where \(\mathbf{D}\) is the hydrodynamic dispersion tensor combining molecular diffusion and mechanical dispersion.

Implementing Darcy Flow in GPU.js

To solve Darcy's law in GPU.js, the rock domain is discretized into a 2D or 3D Cartesian grid using the Finite Difference Method (FDM).

Because the steady-state flow equation is elliptic, it requires an iterative solver. In GPU.js, the Jacobi iteration method is the most straightforward to implement because every cell's state at iteration \(k+1\) depends solely on the neighbor cells from iteration \(k\):

  1. Define matrices for hydraulic conductivity \(K\), initial head \(h\), and boundary conditions (Dirichlet or Neumann).
  2. Create a GPU.js kernel that updates each cell's head value by averaging the hydraulic conductivities and heads of its four (in 2D) or six (in 3D) orthogonal neighbors.
  3. Enable the pipeline: true mode in GPU.js to keep intermediate texture data on the GPU memory between iterations, preventing CPU-GPU memory transfer bottlenecks.
  4. Iterate until the residual head difference drops below an acceptable convergence threshold.

Computing Solute Transport

Once the flow field is established, computing solute transport is an explicit parabolic problem that maps directly to the parallel architecture of GPU.js.

The spatial derivatives for dispersion are approximated using central differences, while advective terms require upwind differencing schemes to prevent non-physical numerical oscillations and instability. A GPU.js kernel takes the velocity output from the Darcy solver, the dispersion coefficients, and the current concentration state to compute concentration updates across explicit time increments \(\Delta t\):

  1. The kernel queries adjacent grid points to determine concentration gradients.
  2. The advection-dispersion flux is calculated per cell.
  3. The new concentration \(C^{t+\Delta t}\) is written to an output texture.
  4. The simulation swaps buffers using a ping-pong approach across time steps to model plume evolution continuously.

Representing Heterogeneous Rock Properties

Real geological media are rarely homogeneous. GPU.js handles spatial heterogeneity by passing 2D or 3D matrices containing localized rock properties directly into kernels:

  • Porosity Fields: Variations in rock storage capacity and pore throat distribution.
  • Permeability Fields: Spatially variable matrices representing fractures, shale barriers, or variable-grain sandstones.
  • Retardation and Sorption: Additional matrices mapping linear adsorption (\(K_d\)) can be integrated directly into the transport step to simulate reactive transport.

Capabilities and Technical Limitations

GPU.js provides massive parallel throughput compared to single-threaded CPU JavaScript, often achieving 10x to 100x speedups for regular grid problems. It makes interactive subsurface contaminant modeling accessible directly inside client web applications.

However, several limitations must be considered:

  • Precision Limits: GPU.js relies primarily on 32-bit floating-point precision (FP32) due to WebGL constraints. Highly sensitive iterative solvers may suffer from cumulative round-off errors.
  • Convergence Rates: While Jacobi iterations parallelize cleanly on GPU threads, they converge much slower than advanced sequential algorithms such as Conjugate Gradient or Multigrid solvers.
  • Memory Bounds: Web browser context limits the size of allocated textures, constraining the maximum grid resolution (typically grids larger than \(2048 \times 2048\) in 2D or \(256 \times 256 \times 256\) in 3D require careful memory profiling).
