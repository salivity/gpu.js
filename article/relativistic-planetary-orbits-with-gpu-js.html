Relativistic Planetary Orbits with GPU.js

Computing planetary orbit trajectories with general relativistic corrections is fully achievable using gpu.js. By compiling JavaScript functions into WebGL-accelerated shader code, gpu.js enables high-performance parallel computation directly in the browser or Node.js. While general relativity introduces non-linear mathematical complexity beyond classical Newtonian mechanics, these equations boil down to arithmetic evaluations that GPUs excel at processing, particularly when simulating multi-body systems or massive ensembles of test particles.

The Physics: Implementing Relativistic Corrections

Classical orbital mechanics relies on Newton’s law of universal gravitation, which fails to account for phenomena such as the anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury. To incorporate general relativity without solving the full Einstein field equations numerically, astrophysicists use the Post-Newtonian (PN) approximation.

For most planetary orbital simulations, the first Post-Newtonian correction (1PN) is sufficient. The acceleration \(\vec{a}\) of a planet incorporates an extra relativistic perturbation term:

\[\vec{a} = -\frac{GM}{r^3}\vec{r} \left( 1 + \frac{3GM}{r c^2} \right)\]

where \(G\) is the gravitational constant, \(M\) is the solar mass, \(r\) is the radial distance, and \(c\) is the speed of light. Because this adjustment relies purely on local vectors (position and velocity) and standard scalar arithmetic, it can be written directly into a gpu.js kernel.

How GPU.js Handles the Computation

gpu.js operates by translating a subset of JavaScript into WebGL or WebGPU shader language (GLSL). Orbital mechanics calculations are executed over grid structures:

  1. Kernel Definition: You define a kernel function that accepts arrays of positions, velocities, and masses.
  2. Force Evaluation: For an \(N\)-body simulation, each thread computes the gravitational pull and relativistic correction exerted on one body by all other bodies (\(O(N^2)\) complexity). The parallel nature of the GPU makes this step significantly faster than running standard CPU loops.
  3. Numerical Integration: Integrators such as Leapfrog, Velocity Verlet, or 4th-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) can be evaluated. Symplectic integrators like Velocity Verlet are preferred for orbital mechanics because they conserve energy over long timescales.

Architectural Strengths

  • Massive Parallelism for \(N\)-Body Systems: When modeling hundreds or thousands of asteroids, planetesimals, or interacting planetary bodies, GPU acceleration prevents the performance bottlenecks typical of single-threaded JavaScript.
  • Monte Carlo Trajectory Sampling: If calculating the trajectory of a single planet under varying initial relativistic parameters, gpu.js can compute thousands of distinct orbital trajectories concurrently.

Technical Challenges and Limitations

  • Sequential Time-Stepping: GPUs parallelize across spatial dimensions (bodies or particles), not time. Because step \(t+1\) depends strictly on step \(t\), the simulation loop itself must run sequentially, either by repeatedly calling the kernel or iteratively computing mini-batches of time steps inside texture memory.
  • Precision Limits: General relativistic orbital shifts, such as Mercury's precession, occur on minute scales (roughly 43 arcseconds per century). Standard WebGL shaders historically default to 32-bit floating-point precision (highp float), which can introduce numerical drift over millions of simulated iterations. While adequate for short-to-medium-term visualizations and qualitative experiments, high-precision scientific modeling often requires 64-bit precision (FP64), which has limited support across WebGL implementations.
