Accelerating Audio Cross-Correlation with GPU.js

Using gpu.js to accelerate cross-correlation between two high-frequency acoustic sensor feeds is technically feasible and can deliver significant speedups over native JavaScript, but its practical viability depends on buffer sizes, transfer overhead, and whether computation occurs in the time or frequency domain. While GPU-driven parallelization dramatically outperforms single-threaded CPU loops for brute-force time-domain algorithms, data transfer latencies through WebGL textures and the mathematical dominance of Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithms often make WebAssembly-based CPU pipelines a faster alternative for real-time streaming.

The Computational Challenge

Cross-correlation measures the similarity and time lag between two signals by continuously shifting one array over another, multiplying overlapping elements, and summing the results. High-frequency acoustic sensors (such as ultrasonic or high-definition hydrophone arrays) often sample at rates between 96 kHz and 1 MHz.

In the time domain, a direct sliding-window cross-correlation has a computational complexity of \(\mathcal{O}(N^2)\), where \(N\) is the sample window size. At high sampling rates, processing these arrays in pure JavaScript introduces severe execution delays, causing audio dropouts and making real-time sensor processing impossible.

How GPU.js Helps

gpu.js compiles written JavaScript functions into WebGL shader language (GLSL), offloading computation directly to the graphics hardware.

For time-domain cross-correlation, gpu.js provides substantial speedups:

  • Massive Parallelism: Computing the correlation at each discrete time delay (\(\tau\)) is an independent operation. The GPU can compute thousands of delay offsets simultaneously across its compute cores.
  • Ease of Implementation: Writing a direct correlation kernel in gpu.js requires only standard JavaScript syntax, abstracting away complex raw WebGL boilerplate.

Critical Bottlenecks to Consider

Despite the raw parallel performance of graphics hardware, several architectural limitations affect gpu.js in high-frequency acoustic workflows:

  1. CPU-to-GPU Data Bus Overhead: gpu.js relies on WebGL, which requires packing array data into textures and pushing it across the system memory bus to the GPU. For continuous, high-frequency streaming data, the constant round-trip latency of uploading fresh sensor buffers and reading back results can easily eclipse the execution time gained.
  2. Algorithmic Disadvantage (Time vs. Frequency Domain): Direct cross-correlation scales poorly (\(\mathcal{O}(N^2)\)). The standard approach for large signals is calculating cross-correlation via the Wiener–Khinchin theorem using FFTs (\(\mathcal{O}(N \log N)\)). Implementing a multi-pass FFT pipeline in gpu.js is complex and incurs significant texture-swapping overhead.
  3. Floating-Point Precision: High-frequency signal analysis often relies on high dynamic range and 32-bit or 64-bit float precision. WebGL implementations on mobile devices or certain browsers may restrict precision, leading to rounding errors in accumulated sums.

Practical Verdict

gpu.js can effectively accelerate acoustic cross-correlation if you are processing discrete batches of data using a direct time-domain approach where window sizes are moderate (a few thousand samples) and precision requirements are standard.

However, for low-latency, real-time streaming of high-frequency acoustic feeds, a frequency-domain approach using WebAssembly (Wasm) combined with SIMD instructions—or migrating to modern WebGPU compute shaders—will generally provide superior throughput and lower latency than gpu.js.
