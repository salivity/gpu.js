Real-Time Color Histograms and CCVs in GPU.js

Generating chromaticity histograms and color-coherence vectors (CCVs) from live camera feeds is achievable in gpu.js by streaming media through WebRTC and processing individual video frames via WebGL fragment shaders. While simple per-pixel transformations such as chromaticity conversion are natively optimal for the parallel architecture of gpu.js, features requiring spatial grouping and global data aggregation—like CCVs and histogram binning—require specialized multi-pass shader pipelines to overcome the absence of atomic write operations in standard WebGL.

To capture the live stream, an HTML5 <video> element receiving data via navigator.mediaDevices.getUserMedia serves as the dynamic input texture. The gpu.js library accepts HTML video elements directly, updating the GPU-bound texture on every requestAnimationFrame loop without requiring CPU-side frame decoding.

Generating a chromaticity histogram begins with mapping the live RGB stream into normalized chromaticity coordinates (\(r = R / (R+G+B)\), \(g = G / (R+G+B)\)) to achieve luminance invariance. Because WebGL fragment shaders cannot perform scattered writes into arbitrary memory bins, calculating the global histogram within gpu.js typically requires a reduction technique. A quantization kernel first discretizes each pixel into a predefined \((r, g)\) bin index. A series of downsampling reduction passes then aggregates these bin counts progressively across the frame, producing the final histogram array.

Color-coherence vectors extend the chromaticity histogram by categorizing pixels as either coherent (belonging to a large continuous region of the same color) or incoherent (isolated pixels or small clusters). This requires connected-component labeling (CCL), which is computationally intensive for real-time applications. Within gpu.js, CCL is executed through iterative label-propagation kernels using the pipeline: true configuration, preventing expensive CPU-GPU data transfers between iterations. Each step compares a pixel’s quantized color to its neighbors, spreading a unique component identifier until convergence is achieved.

Once regions are labeled, a secondary pass evaluates the component sizes against a coherence threshold (typically \(\tau \ge 1\%\) of total pixels). Pixels are then tagged as coherent or incoherent, yielding two distinct histograms per color bin.

Practical real-time execution in the browser requires balancing video resolution and pipeline depth. Performing multi-pass reductions and iterative CCL on standard HD resolutions will lead to dropped frames. Scaling down the incoming camera feed to a lower processing resolution (such as 160x120 or 320x240) preserves dominant spatial coherence and chromatic signatures while maintaining a stable 30 to 60 frames per second.
